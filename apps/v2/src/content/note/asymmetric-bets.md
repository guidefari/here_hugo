The clearest account of this idea comes from Dax Raad in Egghead's [Developer Career Growth and Asymmetric Bets](https://egghead.io/developer-career-growth-and-asymmetric-bets-with-dax-raad~nzzun). The episode is a good starting point: he describes bets with low downside and huge potential upside, using SST as the example.

## The idea

An asymmetric bet has a capped loss and an open-ended gain. For a builder, the important question is not only, “Will this work?” It is also, **“What do I keep if it does not?”**

A good failed project can leave behind:

- a skill that transfers to the next project;
- code, a library, or a public demo;
- a clearer view of a market or problem;
- reputation, collaborators, or an audience; and
- another option you could not see before.

That makes the real downside smaller than the time and money spent. Failure still moves you forward.

> Choose work that makes you stronger when it fails, freer when it succeeds, and never fragile enough that one miss takes you out of the game.

## What this looks like

Dax's version is not “take wild risks.” It is a way to choose projects.

- **Learn outside your lane.** Do not make one framework, employer, or hiring market your only option. Unusual or adjacent skills create rare combinations.
- **Make work public when it makes sense.** Open source, writing, and demos can teach you, show your work, bring users, and lead to future work at once.
- **Take risks that leave you with something.** Dax experiments with new technology so that even a failed product can leave him with useful skills, research, and better judgment for the next project.
- **Work close to the problem.** Building tools you use gives fast feedback and keeps the work honest.
- **Check the ceiling.** A project can work and still be a poor bet if the market or ownership structure caps its best outcome. Dax's decision to move on from [Bumi](https://withbumi.com/) is a useful example; the market was too small.
- **Count the ongoing cost.** Cheap code is not cheap software. Every feature can add maintenance, support, and complexity.

## A simple test

Before starting, ask:

1. Can I afford to lose the time and money?
2. What useful thing will remain if this fails?
3. Does success create ownership, reach, or more choices?
4. Is there a real path to a much larger outcome?
5. Can I stop before the project becomes a permanent burden?

The aim is not to predict the winner. It is to keep taking survivable shots where a win can matter a lot.

## A barbell, not a lottery ticket

Keep your foundation safe, then use a fixed amount of time or money to try projects with much larger upside. Never risk your health, income, or ability to keep working on one project.

The useful connection to [Naval Ravikant](https://nav.al/rich) is this: build skills and assets that remain useful. A developer's failed project can still leave behind better skills, reusable code, users, or reputation—even if it never becomes a business.

## Related

Jesse Hanley, the founder of Bento, puts it this way: do the hardest thing. He means choosing a valuable problem that most people will avoid, then staying with it. The point is not to seek difficulty for its own sake. It is to choose a problem with room to grow. Justin Jackson recounts the idea in [The Hard Thing](https://justinjackson.ca/hard-thing).
