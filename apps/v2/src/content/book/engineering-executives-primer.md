## Chapter 9: Managing Your Priorities and Energy

### Company, team, self

Larson's [company, team, self](https://lethain.com/company-team-self/) framework puts the company's needs first, the team's second, and your own third. It helps resolve competing priorities, but he's stopped recommending it without qualification. It's too reductive to follow this alone, esp if you repeatedly follow the right priorities in a way that leaves people burnt out.

**When it works:**

- When personal career goals or team preferences are displacing work the company actually needs.
- When teams need a shared way to resolve trade-offs beyond their own interests.
- When doing the highest-priority work also leaves people with enough energy to keep going.

> I still believe it’s conceptually good advice and I continue to see managers who fail because they are missing this perspective. However, I’ve also seen some of the best leaders that I’ve worked with burn out by following this advice too loyally.


**When it doesn't:**

- When "self last" becomes a standing rule for neglecting your own energy. A sustainable career needs both impact and engagement.
- When it rules out all lower-priority work, even work that restores motivation and helps people accomplish more overall.
- When a leader assumes everyone finds strict adherence to the "correct" priorities as energizing as they do.

The exception has a limit: energizing work shouldn't create problems for other teams. Exploring a new language in a throwaway prototype is different from putting it into production and leaving others to support it.

### Highlights


> it became challenging to distinguish whether any particular platform was principally anchored to business impact or career progression. In that environment, pushing the team to explicitly prioritize the company and team first was extremely valuable.

> I frequently noticed cases where engineers did the best thing for the company, solving an urgent problem with little staffing, but weren’t recognized or promoted for their work. Those engineers would slowly become de-energized and frustrated.

> a durable approach would require balancing the company’s priorities with the need to remain personally energized for the long haul.

> folks will accomplish more if you let them do some energizing work, even if some of that work isn’t very important.

> Rigid adherence to any prioritization model, even one that’s conceptually correct like mine that put the company and team first, will often lead to the right list of priorities but a team that’s got too little energy to make forward progress.

> modestly violating priorities to energize your team in pursuit of a broader goal is an open leadership secret

> What folks may not understand is that, for a certain type of person, strictly adhering to the correct path is very energizing

> However, I admit that I’ve never really seen high-adherence personalities succeed in senior engineering roles

> Blunting my desire for adherence has been one of the most valuable lessons I’ve learned over the last couple decades.

## Chapter 10: Meetings for an Effective Engineering Organization

> keep your team moving together toward the same goals—what’s really important right now?

> If you’ve not read Patrick Lencioni’s The Five Dysfunctions of a Team (Jossey-Bass), it’s a quick, remarkable read on this topic.)

> I’d strongly push the core Engineering leadership team to meet weekly. I would only reduce that frequency at very small companies (<20 engineers)

> Reading the written document should be a prerequisite to providing feedback on it

> Bad reviews are anchored to the author presenting their document

> and it’s certainly a bad sign when you are the loudest voice in either forum

> The team will watch you closely and take cues from your behavior. Even if you say a meeting is important, they’ll only believe you if you show up regularly

## Chapter 11: Internal Communications

> you can close the distance between yourself and the team by sending a short, weekly email. This is a good opportunity to let folks know what you’re focused on and what you’re doing

> The most frequent issue I see is when a literal communicator insists on engaging in the details with a less literal executive. I call the remedy “extracting the kernel.”

> The team presenting to the CEO could rightly argue that executives ought to be better communicators. That’s certainly true, but the reality is that executives are human. You’ll make much more progress by focusing on improving how you communicate with them than by blaming them for their deficiencies

## Chapter 13: Working with Your CEO, Peers, and Engineering

> When a new Engineering executive is hired, they are usually brought in by a CEO who believes that the Engineering function is underperforming. When you talk to members of Engineering, they will likely have a different perspective, potentially that the CEO keeps changing direction too frequently. When you talk to peer executives and the board of directors, you will hear a third and a fourth narrative about what’s happening

> Successful executives debug these mismatched concerns until they merge into a single cohesive perspective

> Even if you’re an executive who prioritizes supporting your team, it’s very hard not to come into your new job anchored to your CEO’s evaluation of how Engineering is performing

## Chapter 14: Gelling Your Engineering Leadership Team

> Most leaders have a strong desire to perform, and establishing

> clear expectations sends the message that you’re here to help them perform.

> When it’s possible, it’s well worth your time to consider having one or two senior engineers to report to you, as opposed to only managing Engineering managers

> A transactional collection of individuals can do good work, but a gelled group that knows each other is better positioned to work through the inevitable difficult moments.

> For senior leaders, I recommend giving the problem back to them: the next step in their career is being an executive, and certainly no one is going to tell them how to advance their scope at that point. Particularly, encourage them to look at opportunities to grow themselves and their career in ways that aren’t zero-sum

## Chapter 15: Building Your Network

> The best way to meet founders is to work in fast-growing, entrepreneurial companies. The second best way is angel investing. A small amount of angel investing goes a surprisingly long way

> If the slow approach isn’t working for you, I particularly recommend putting your CEO to work connecting you with a few venture capitalists they know, likely on your board, which will help you better understand their perspectives on your company, in addition to growing your network
