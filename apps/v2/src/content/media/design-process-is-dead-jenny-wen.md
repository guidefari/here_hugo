Jenny Wen leads design for Claude at Anthropic. She previously led design at Figma, including FigJam and Slides, and worked at Dropbox, Square, and Shopify.

## Why the Design Process Is Dead

The discovery, mock, iterate, and converge process no longer leads product development. Claude Code lets engineers make working prototypes before a designer can finish a polished mockup.

Design now has two jobs:

1. **Support implementation.** Engineers ship rough versions quickly; designers provide last-mile polish and work in code with them.
2. **Set direction.** Teams still need a shared target, but the horizon is now three to six months. A working prototype is often more useful than a multi-year vision deck.

## Hiring for Design and Engineering Teams

Adaptability matters alongside craft. Wen's three archetypes apply to teams whose roles are stretching across design, product, and engineering.

1. **Strong generalists.** "Block-shaped" people are strong across several core skills, not merely competent outside one specialty.
2. **Deep specialists.** Exceptional technical or visual depth still differentiates the work when more people can make a first version.
3. **Exceptional new graduates.** Fast learners without inherited rituals can adapt quickly. Build and ship real things instead of relying on theoretical exercises.

## Management Principles

- **Do the seemingly low-leverage work.** Dogfood the product, reproduce bugs, read logs with engineers, and land the occasional PR. It builds trust and product judgment.
- **Earn psychological safety.** A team that can tease its leader may feel safe enough to speak plainly. Care personally, maintain high standards, and do not confuse management with friendship.
- **Stay close to the work.** Managers need firsthand experience with new tools to guide teams through change credibly.

[Evan Tana's framework](https://x.com/evantana/status/1927404374252269667) places ideas by founder legibility and idea legibility. The interesting area is an unclear idea with real energy: it may be frontier work, poorly explained work, or both.

For a designer, the task is to spot those ideas and give them form through storytelling, UX, or a prototype. That makes the framework useful for deciding which unclear ideas deserve attention.

This resembles [asymmetric bets](/asymmetric-bets/): take survivable shots where the upside can be large, even before the outcome is obvious.
