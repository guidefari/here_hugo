Research cutoff and retrieval: November 8, 2026. Original operator documentation, changelogs and public discussion comments were fetched. Researcher findings are attributed, not independently reproduced. Existing maintainer notes were read for context. No registry publishing or security testing was performed.

## What do scanning, dual-use policy and support actually require?

### Takeaway

Publish-time scanning introduces a documented availability delay, not merely a CLI slowdown. The operator's typical five-minute estimate is explicitly not a service guarantee. There is strong primary evidence of much longer individual delays and broken coordinated releases, but no representative latency distribution. Importantly, current dual-use policy is stricter than the launch changelog's OIDC wording.

### Cited Findings

- **July 28, 2026 launch:** newly published packages are scanned before installation availability. Outcomes include publication, manual-review hold or blocking. GitHub states "typically around five minutes", potentially "up to 15 minutes or more" at peaks or depending on size/content, explicitly saying these are current typical behavior, may change and "aren't a service guarantee". Do not convert 15 minutes into a maximum or call five minutes an independently measured median. [Official launch](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-07-28-npm-publish-time-malware-scanning-and-dual-use-metadata/).
- While pending, `npm dist-tag` continues to work, but version-dependent `npm deprecate` and `npm unpublish` do not work until availability. The PM recommends polling/retrying availability rather than sleeping for a fixed period. On August 3 the PM said all packages, private and public, are subject to scanning. This means private packages on npm, not proof that npm controls independently operated registries. [Launch](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-07-28-npm-publish-time-malware-scanning-and-dual-use-metadata/); [PM discussion](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/203413); [private/public clarification](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/203413#discussioncomment-17884934).
- **Exact dual-use metadata:** `"contentPolicy": { "class": "dual-use" }` in `package.json`, plus a free-form text `DISCLOSURE` at the published tarball root describing functionality and legitimate intended use. Declaration is not automatic permission to publish; additional scanning and case-by-case Trust & Safety review remain possible. Both must persist across versions; removing the declaration requires Trust & Safety review. Enforcement is progressive, with direct emails to affected maintainers. Examples include penetration-testing, security research and obfuscation tools, not all ordinary JavaScript packages. [Current policy, marked updated July 28](https://docs.npmjs.com/policies/dual-use).
- **Material official-source conflict:** the July changelog lists trusted publishing/OIDC among examples of 2FA-enforced publishing. The current policy expressly says **direct OIDC publishing is not permitted for dual-use packages**. Direct publishing requires an interactive 2FA-authenticated session; OIDC or bypass-2FA granular tokens may stage, followed by human 2FA promotion. Use the current policy for present-tense requirements and flag the launch wording as inconsistent. Neither the existence nor effective date of a later policy revision was established from page history. [Launch wording](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-07-28-npm-publish-time-malware-scanning-and-dual-use-metadata/); [explicit current prohibition](https://docs.npmjs.com/policies/dual-use#publishing-methods-that-are-not-permitted).
- **Appeal promises are qualified:** launch says blocked publishers "may receive a notification with the option to appeal", and possible maintainer-account action depends on finding confidence/severity. On August 3 npm PM Leo Balter said long-delay notifications exist and maintainers receive direct support links for suspected false positives. Neither source commits to a review deadline, successful restoration or guaranteed scan completion time. [Launch](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-07-28-npm-publish-time-malware-scanning-and-dual-use-metadata/); [PM reply](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/203413#discussioncomment-17884917).
- **October 15 concrete appeal account:** Allan Bowe reports five removed `@sasjs/adapter` versions, including one with metadata, disclosure and staged 2FA promotion. He reports unexplained removal notices and an appeal link apparently landing at the generic product picker. Balter replies that selecting the associated account/org is the expected step before the reinstatement form, acknowledges the confusing experience, and says: "we don't currently share the underlying findings in removal notices, and we aren't planning to change that." He says retaining dual-use classification across releases is work in progress, not resolution of the reported removals. This verifies opacity and an operator response, not an independently adjudicated false positive. [Publisher report](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/203413#discussioncomment-18947552); [operator response](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/203413#discussioncomment-18955930).
- **Status at rollout:** July 30 tooling feedback asks how to distinguish pending scanning from never published; an August 3 comment relays `npm view` as the workaround, while Balter says appropriate scanning status is prioritized. September roadmap says staged-version history and scan-gated approval have since shipped. Avoid repeating the July status gap as proof that no visibility improvements existed by November. A complete programmatic scan-status API was not verified. [Tooling feedback](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/203413#discussioncomment-17843611); [PM status reply](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/203413#discussioncomment-17884911); [roadmap](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/208130).
- **Effect, October 11:** Sebastian Lorenz (`fubhy`) reports Effect 4.0.3 stuck in validation for 22 hours, regularly several-hour to day-long delays, and inconsistent core-ecosystem versions harming downstream upgrades. He reports Changesets/staging compatibility problems, rejects manually approving 20 to 30-plus packages and describes mostly unsuccessful support tickets, emails and social contact, while crediting Ashley Willis's escalations. These are first-person observations and assessments, not telemetry or a universal npm support failure. [Original detailed account](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/208130#discussioncomment-18860239).
- **Cloudflare's own operational evidence, August 13:** Pete Bacon Darwin reports `wrangler` marked latest while its `miniflare` dependency remained unavailable, breaking `npx wrangler@latest`. His initial objection that OIDC could not manage dist-tags was valid for his dated workflow, but on October 5 he acknowledges OIDC dist-tagging is now available and says they implemented dependency-tiered publishing instead. This is particularly relevant to an article proposing Cloudflare as operator. [August report](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/203413#discussioncomment-17999689); [October update and mitigation](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/203413#discussioncomment-18762490).
- **Other synchronized-release evidence:** AWS SDK maintainer `kuhe` requests ordering support for up to hundreds of packages per day on October 5; `Tobbe` describes mixed latest versions across a 70-plus-package monorepo on September 19, using temporary tags then promotion to shrink the inconsistency window. These are concrete scope-specific accounts, not an ecosystem-wide failure rate. [AWS](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/203413#discussioncomment-18761755); [monorepo workaround](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/208130#discussioncomment-18517304).
- npm explicitly describes scanning as another layer, "not a guarantee that every malicious package will be detected". Jovi De Croock criticizes missed malware and asks for external validators, but these comments do not themselves prove a particular known malicious hash was admitted after being known to npm. [Operator caveat](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/208130); [maintainer critique](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/208130#discussioncomment-18549814).

### Inferences

- Article-safe framing: security hardening has imposed a release-coordination cost that distribution/CDN speed alone cannot fix. The evidence supports better availability signaling, dependency-aware promotion, batch review and accessible escalation as competitive requirements, not removing all security controls. [Cloudflare report](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/203413#discussioncomment-17999689); [Effect report](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/208130#discussioncomment-18860239).
- Polling, temporary tags and dependency-tiered publishing mitigate particular automation failures, but do not constitute an atomic multi-package transaction or a guarantee against manual-review holds. [PM guidance](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/203413); [temporary-tag account](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/208130#discussioncomment-18517304).

### Gaps

- No independent latency percentile distribution, scan false-positive/negative rate, complete status-API audit or support/appeal turnaround SLA was established. Five minutes is an operator estimate; 22 hours is one maintainer's dated report.
- The specific allegation that scanning misses already-known hashes remains unverified here. It needs exact malicious versions/hashes, dates of publication/detection, and evidence of what npm knew then. Do not substitute a comment about missed attacks for that proof.
- No final resolution of Effect's October delay or Bowe's removals was established. No account-recovery incident involving the named maintainers was established.

## What has npm shipped, and what exactly was SlugFest?

### Takeaway

Trusted publishing is an existing npm feature, not an obvious missing invention for a competitor. SlugFest has a recoverable original researcher report and corroborating official mitigation, but its scope is **never-used, unvalidated trust configurations**, not every trusted publisher. The 48-hour expiry narrows exposure rather than resolving immutable identity when the configuration is saved.

### Cited Findings

- **GA July 31, 2025:** supported public/private and scoped/unscoped npm packages using GitHub-hosted Actions or GitLab.com shared runners, npm CLI 11.5.1+. Contemporary docs add CircleCI cloud, require Node 22.14.0+, and still exclude self-hosted runners. Provenance and publishing authentication are distinct: automatic provenance requires a public package and public source repository on GitHub Actions/GitLab; CircleCI provenance is unsupported. Private dependency installation still needs separate authentication, for which docs recommend read-only granular tokens. [GA original](https://github.blog/changelog/2025-07-31-npm-trusted-publishing-with-oidc-is-generally-available/); [current docs](https://docs.npmjs.com/trusted-publishers).
- **Token history:** September 29, 2025 announcement specifies seven-day default and 90-day maximum for newly created write-enabled granular tokens, not compulsory weekly rotation for everyone. Its mid-November classic-token sunset was a plan. Actual permanent classic-token revocation was announced December 9, along with two-hour login sessions, publish 2FA, CLI granular-token management and 2FA defaults for new packages. Existing packages retained their 2FA settings. The December post reiterates a 90-day write-token maximum and temporarily restores a legacy endpoint to mitigate Yarn v1/v2 compatibility problems. [September plan](https://github.blog/changelog/2025-09-29-strengthening-npm-security-important-changes-to-authentication-and-token-management/); [actual completion](https://github.blog/changelog/2025-12-09-npm-classic-tokens-revoked-session-based-auth-and-cli-token-management-now-available/).
- **Still future at cutoff:** the September 17, 2026 roadmap says bypass-2FA granular tokens lose direct publishing in January 2027, not that all granular tokens or all CI automation have already been banned. Stage-only tokens plus human 2FA promotion are the fallback; direct OIDC remains possible for eligible packages/workflows. It records existing sensitive-write restrictions and 72-hour holds following recovery-code sign-in. Unsupported providers are acknowledged as a real gap, and staging is expressly not parity with native OIDC. [Operator roadmap](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/208130).
- **Already-shipped mitigations:** roadmap dates staged-publishing GA to May 22 and multiple trusted publishers to September 3, 2026. Current docs allow up to 10 connections per package, independent stage/direct-publish/tag permissions, and opt-in OIDC dist-tag operations on npm 11.21.0+ or 12.2.0+. `npm trust` already supports configuring multiple packages, per the PM's September 17 response. Staged new-package creation now publishes a public `0.0.0-stage` placeholder while keeping the intended staged version private until approval. [Roadmap](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/208130); [OIDC docs](https://docs.npmjs.com/trusted-publishers); [staging docs](https://docs.npmjs.com/staged-publishing); [CLI correction](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/208130#discussioncomment-18489512).
- **Remaining friction:** stage list/view/approve/reject require interactive authentication, not OIDC. Batch approval/publishing plans are roadmap priorities, not dated commitments; users still request them October 29. September 29 enterprise feedback reports trusted publishing failing after migration to GitHub data-residency hosting; this is a specific `.ghe.com` account, not proof every GitHub Enterprise product is unsupported. [OIDC docs](https://docs.npmjs.com/trusted-publishers); [batch request](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/208130#discussioncomment-19264806); [enterprise report](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/208130#discussioncomment-18667148).
- **Original SlugFest researcher:** Golan Myers of Bloom Security says npm stored owner/repository slug, workflow basename and optional environment, initially without binding numeric repository/owner IDs. Those IDs were recorded only after first successful publication. The attack requires an unused trust binding pointing to a slug the attacker can actually reclaim. Bloom reports an end-to-end proof using its own npm scope and repositories under different organizations, not a victim-package compromise. [Original researcher writeup](https://bloom.security/blog/slugfest-millions-of-ai-agents-download-exposed-npm-packages).
- **Exposure is not exploitation:** Bloom reports 1,500-plus vulnerable packages, over 10 million cumulative and over one million monthly downloads. These are vendor-reported exposure counts, not 1,500 hijacked packages, malware downloads or confirmed victims. A valid provenance attestation could identify the attacker's source repository, so checking that provenance exists is weaker than checking expected identity. Bloom also reports GitLab affected. No independently reproduced package census or exploitation-in-the-wild evidence was obtained. [Bloom](https://bloom.security/blog/slugfest-millions-of-ai-agents-download-exposed-npm-packages).
- **Official October 2 mitigation:** unvalidated configurations expire 48 hours after creation and cease authorizing publication. First successful publish validates them and exempts them from expiry; identity changes require a new trust relationship and window. Expired entries remain visible without counting against limits; other valid connections are unaffected. GitHub also blocks `issue_comment` OIDC publishing alongside existing `pull_request_target` restrictions, recommending `push`, `release` or `workflow_dispatch`. Current npm docs explicitly say first publish binds immutable repository identity and that configuration is not verified on save. [Official change](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-10-02-unvalidated-npm-trusted-publishing-configurations-now-expire/); [current binding docs](https://docs.npmjs.com/trusted-publishers#trusted-publisher-configuration-expiry).
- **Disclosure chronology, attributed:** Bloom dates submission June 6, triage June 9, last substantive reply July 8, six subsequent unanswered enquiries including mediation, and discovery of October 2 mitigation on retest before publication. It says npm apologized for communication. The article body displays October 13; fetched metadata displays October 28. WorkOS's October 16 technical explainer identifies October 13 as disclosure and correctly distinguishes a shortened window from identity resolution at trust creation. WorkOS is a secondary explainer, not the discovering researcher. [Bloom](https://bloom.security/blog/slugfest-millions-of-ai-agents-download-exposed-npm-packages); [WorkOS](https://workos.com/blog/npm-trusted-publishing-slugfest).

### Inferences

- Use: "Bloom disclosed a slug-reclaim flaw affecting unvalidated npm trusted-publisher configurations; GitHub imposed 48-hour expiry, while identity binding still occurs at first publish." Do not say npm never uses immutable IDs, that all existing validated repositories are reclaimable, or that nothing was fixed. [Bloom](https://bloom.security/blog/slugfest-millions-of-ai-agents-download-exposed-npm-packages); [official mitigation](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-10-02-unvalidated-npm-trusted-publishing-configurations-now-expire/).
- The remaining conceptual window is creation to first publish, at most 48 hours, not indefinite unused authorizations. Auditing/removing stale bindings and publishing promptly after setup are Bloom's recommended mitigations. Neither this flaw nor provider restrictions invalidate the general advantage of reducing reusable CI secrets. [Bloom](https://bloom.security/blog/slugfest-millions-of-ai-agents-download-exposed-npm-packages); [current guidance](https://docs.npmjs.com/trusted-publishers).

### Gaps

- No dedicated official CVE/GHSA or complete SlugFest incident advisory was located in this research. The official changelog corroborates the identity-risk mitigation but does not endorse Bloom's counts or full disclosure timeline.
- Treatment of configurations already unvalidated on October 2, exact current residual exploitable population and independent testing of GitLab behavior were not established. Do not describe all 1,500 as still exposed on November 8.
- No completed foundation-wide CI compatibility audit or guaranteed date for self-hosted support was obtained. Future-direction language is not a delivery commitment.

## What do unpublishing and JSR comparison actually establish?

### Takeaway

npm's voluntary unpublish policy has two eligibility paths, not a blanket 72-hour deadline and not a single giant AND condition. Deprecation preserves installation and cannot be treated as removal. JSR has its own moderation, operator and security responsibilities; its documentation does not justify presenting it as an automatically safer or scanning-free escape.

### Cited Findings

- **Exact policy logic:** a newly created package may be unpublished within its first 72 hours **if no other public-registry packages depend on it**. Separately, **regardless of age**, unpublication is allowed if **all three** hold: no public-registry dependents, **less than 300 downloads over the last week**, and **a single owner/maintainer**. Thus the low-impact path is available even beyond 72 hours; 300 is exclusive, not inclusive. The text speaks of newly created packages for the first path, not an unconditional fresh 72-hour allowance for every new version of an established package. [Official unpublish policy](https://docs.npmjs.com/policies/unpublish).
- **Identity and irreversibility:** a used `package@version` can never be published again, even after unpublish. Single-version and whole-package unpublish exist; unpublish is irreversible. Entirely removing all versions creates a 24-hour wait before publishing new versions under that package name. [Policy](https://docs.npmjs.com/policies/unpublish).
- **Deprecate versus remove:** `npm deprecate` leaves a version downloadable and adds an author-written warning; deprecating the whole package drops its name from search results. It does not free its version identity, withdraw the tarball or prevent dependent builds from using it. "Yank" should not be used as a synonym without specifying registry semantics: npm's cited policy describes deprecation and unpublication, not a separate Cargo-style yank operation. [Policy](https://docs.npmjs.com/policies/unpublish).
- **Security takedown is distinct:** the policy separately directs terms/policy violations to support and copyright claims to DMCA procedures; scanning launch reserves blocking and account action. The voluntary author's low-impact thresholds are therefore not evidence that npm cannot remove popular malware. [Policy reporting path](https://docs.npmjs.com/policies/unpublish#issues); [security enforcement](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-07-28-npm-publish-time-malware-scanning-and-dual-use-metadata/).
- **JSR acceptable use and discretionary power:** its usage policy, marked March 6, 2024, bans malware, harmful/deceptive packages and scope squatting, permits legitimate malware-analysis tools and reserves account suspension/termination and reclaiming infringing names. It provides package-page reporting and states policies may change. This is governance and enforcement surface, not proof of abuse or absent security. [JSR policy](https://jsr.io/docs/usage-policy).
- **JSR governance is transitional in its own current text:** it says Deno currently operates JSR, names a community board and describes a future foundation move. Moderation-group responsibilities include policy and appeals, but the page also says the group will be established after board formation, despite listing board members. Treat that internal mixture of current and future language cautiously; do not report completed foundation transfer from this page. [JSR governance](https://jsr.io/docs/governance).
- **JSR security claims have limits:** docs describe automatic SLSA provenance for native GitHub Actions publishing, an opt-out flag, and manifest/npm-tarball publish attestations as not yet implemented. These are specific controls and documented gaps, not comparative malware-detection benchmarks. [JSR provenance docs](https://jsr.io/docs/trust).

### Inferences

- Registry immutability protects reproducibility but imposes real correction costs; npm deliberately recommends warnings rather than deletion where removal would break dependents. That is a policy tradeoff, not inherently registry neglect. [npm rationale](https://docs.npmjs.com/policies/unpublish).
- A rival must define how approval, moderation, appeals, immutable artifacts and security incidents work. Neither open source nor a different company automatically eliminates those trust decisions. JSR's published controls and governance are a useful comparison, not proof of categorical superiority. [JSR governance](https://jsr.io/docs/governance); [JSR trust](https://jsr.io/docs/trust); [npm policy](https://docs.npmjs.com/policies/unpublish).

### Gaps

- No primary source confirming JSR's current publish-time malware scanner, detection coverage, false-positive rate or review-time SLA was recovered. Absence of such documentation in this search does not establish no scanning.
- No comparative npm/JSR security outcome study, account-recovery success rate or appeals performance dataset was found. Avoid calling JSR safer, less secure or free of npm-style moderation risks without additional evidence.
