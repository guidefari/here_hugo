Research cutoff: November 8, 2026. Sources were searched and the principal sources below were fetched in full. Counts remain attributed snapshots, not a reconstructed global total. Search-only leads are explicitly identified. No article files were changed.

## What happened on September 8, 2025, and what does the download count mean?

### Takeaway
The Qix account takeover and browser cryptocurrency interception are well supported. The frequently repeated billions-per-week figure describes ordinary downloads of package names, not downloads of poisoned versions or a count of affected people. [Datadog retrospective](https://securitylabs.datadoghq.com/articles/learnings-from-recent-npm-compromises/)

### Cited Findings
- Date and entry: September 8, 2025; maintainer Josh Junon, Qix-, received a purported npm 2FA-reset email from `support@npmjs.help`. StepSecurity quotes the maintainer: "It was a 2FA reset email that looked shockingly authentic." It links the original disclosure at [debug issue comment](https://github.com/debug-js/debug/issues/1005#issuecomment-3266868187). The issue page fetch did not render comments, so the quotation was verified in StepSecurity's reproduction, not independently in the comment. [StepSecurity, September 8](https://www.stepsecurity.io/blog/20-popular-npm-packages-compromised-chalk-debug-strip-ansi-color-convert-wrap-ansi)
- The malicious payload intercepted browser network/wallet APIs and substituted attacker-controlled cryptocurrency destinations. This is not the same install-time credential worm as Shai-Hulud. [Datadog](https://securitylabs.datadoghq.com/articles/learnings-from-recent-npm-compromises/); [StepSecurity](https://www.stepsecurity.io/blog/20-popular-npm-packages-compromised-chalk-debug-strip-ansi-color-convert-wrap-ansi)
- The original eighteen package/version pairs enumerated by StepSecurity are: `ansi-styles@6.2.2`, `strip-ansi@7.1.1`, `ansi-regex@6.2.1`, `debug@4.4.2`, `color-convert@3.1.1`, `color-name@2.0.1`, `supports-color@10.2.1`, `chalk@5.6.1`, `wrap-ansi@9.0.1`, `slice-ansi@7.1.1`, `color@5.0.1`, `color-string@2.1.1`, `is-arrayish@0.3.3`, `simple-swizzle@0.2.3`, `supports-hyperlinks@4.1.1`, `has-ansi@6.0.1`, `chalk-template@1.1.1`, and `backslash@0.2.1`. The same article headline says "20+", despite its eighteen-item table. Use "eighteen initially listed packages", not a definitive final campaign total. [StepSecurity](https://www.stepsecurity.io/blog/20-popular-npm-packages-compromised-chalk-debug-strip-ansi-color-convert-wrap-ansi)
- Datadog's October 30 retrospective says "more than 18 npm packages" and "the malicious versions were downloaded more than 2.5 million times". This latter figure is about malicious-version downloads, unlike the package popularity headlines, but the post does not expose a download dataset or counting procedure. Its statement that only $500 was stolen is introduced as "it is reported". Neither should become an independently audited exposure/loss total. [Datadog](https://securitylabs.datadoghq.com/articles/learnings-from-recent-npm-compromises/)
- Search-only corroboration: Snyk's rolling September 9/10 updates add DuckDB and other package names beyond the original Qix list. This is another reason not to compare eighteen and twenty-plus as if they were the same fixed inventory. [Snyk](https://snyk.io/blog/npm-supply-chain-attack-via-open-source-maintainer-compromise/)
- Search-only timing claim: Wiz says takeover was around 9 AM EST, acknowledgement around 11 AM EST, removal "a few hours later", but also describes a "short 2-hour timeframe". That is not a sufficiently precise package-by-package availability record. [Wiz September 9](https://www.wiz.io/blog/widespread-npm-supply-chain-attack-breaking-down-impact-scope-across-debug-chalk)

### Inferences
- Safe article wording: "In September 2025, a phishing attack against an npm maintainer poisoned releases of chalk, debug and other ubiquitous packages with browser-side cryptocurrency-stealing code." Add the exact package versions if discussing actual exposure. [Evidence](https://www.stepsecurity.io/blog/20-popular-npm-packages-compromised-chalk-debug-strip-ansi-color-convert-wrap-ansi)
- Package popularity measures potential distribution reach. Downloads are not unique developers, production deployments, successful payload executions, or wallet thefts. The contrast between the package popularity and malicious-version figures is explicit in the cited reporting. [Evidence](https://securitylabs.datadoghq.com/articles/learnings-from-recent-npm-compromises/)

### Gaps
- No independently verified universal two-hour availability window. Detection, acknowledgement, removal, stale caches and deployed browser bundles are different clocks.
- No reconciled, date-stamped primary npm download export for the conflicting weekly popularity totals, or independently verified campaign-wide wallet-loss figure. Omit exact weekly totals unless the article attributes a specific dated source and calls them package popularity.

## Which Shai-Hulud waves are distinct, and what are defensible dated counts?

### Takeaway
Separate September 2025, November 2025, May 2026 and August 2026. Counts of packages, versions, public exfiltration repositories, exposed credentials and users are different measures and cannot be merged. [GitHub September response](https://github.blog/security/supply-chain-security/our-plan-for-a-more-secure-npm-supply-chain/); [Datadog November analysis](https://securitylabs.datadoghq.com/articles/shai-hulud-2.0-npm-worm/); [Microsoft May update](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2025/12/09/shai-hulud-2-0-guidance-for-detecting-investigating-and-defending-against-the-supply-chain-attack/)

### Cited Findings
- September: GitHub's September 22, 2025 response states, "On September 14, 2025, we were notified of the Shai-Hulud attack" and reports "Immediate removal of 500+ compromised packages" plus blocking uploads carrying its malware indicators. This is an official registry-operator response, not evidence of 500 affected people or a November count. The visible article date is September 22 even though search metadata sometimes gives September 23. [GitHub](https://github.blog/security/supply-chain-security/our-plan-for-a-more-secure-npm-supply-chain/)
- Wiz's September 16 analysis describes malicious versions published September 15, `postinstall` execution, TruffleHog and environment/cloud-secret theft, public GitHub `Shai-Hulud` repositories, malicious workflows and automatic infection/publication using newly found npm credentials. Its proposed connection to the August Nx/s1ngularity incident is explicitly a Wiz assessment based on victimology, not a publicly established single original entry point. [Wiz](https://www.wiz.io/blog/shai-hulud-npm-supply-chain-attack)
- Thus GitHub's September 14 notification, Wiz's September 15 publications, and a retrospective heading calling it September 16 should not be flattened into a falsely precise single start date. [GitHub](https://github.blog/security/supply-chain-security/our-plan-for-a-more-secure-npm-supply-chain/); [Wiz](https://www.wiz.io/blog/shai-hulud-npm-supply-chain-attack); [Datadog retrospective](https://securitylabs.datadoghq.com/articles/learnings-from-recent-npm-compromises/)
- November: Datadog's November 25 analysis, last updated December 4, identifies November 24 community detection and a consolidated, deduplicated cross-vendor list of at least **796 unique package names and 1,092 package versions**. Its over-20-million weekly downloads figure describes package popularity, not poisoned-version downloads. It observed its last affected publication at 18:00 UTC November 24, not necessarily the last anywhere. [Datadog](https://securitylabs.datadoghq.com/articles/shai-hulud-2.0-npm-worm/)
- November changes include `preinstall`, `setup_bun.js`, Bun-executed `bun_environment.js`, cloud credential harvesting, self-hosted GitHub runner persistence, reuse of another victim's exposed GitHub token for exfiltration, and an attempted home-directory destruction fallback. Datadog estimates over 500 unique GitHub users and 150 organizations from public data as a lower bound, while noting repositories and users do not map one-to-one to actual victims. [Datadog](https://securitylabs.datadoghq.com/articles/shai-hulud-2.0-npm-worm/)
- First-person November entry-point evidence: PostHog's November 26 postmortem traces a November 18 external PR exploiting `pull_request_target` plus checkout/execution of attacker-controlled code, theft of a bot PAT, a November 23 workflow-secret theft including an npm token, and malicious package publication at 04:11 UTC November 24. It reports removing its malicious versions and revoking publishing tokens by 09:30 UTC. These are PostHog's timestamps, not the campaign-wide exposure window. [PostHog](https://posthog.com/blog/nov-24-shai-hulud-attack-post-mortem)
- PostHog explicitly says its publishing credentials were not initially stolen by the worm: "We were targeted directly ... to act as a 'patient zero'". Datadog's December 4 update treats this as one of likely multiple initial vectors. Do not claim a unique universal patient zero. [PostHog](https://posthog.com/blog/nov-24-shai-hulud-attack-post-mortem); [Datadog](https://securitylabs.datadoghq.com/articles/shai-hulud-2.0-npm-worm/)
- May 2026: Microsoft's December 9, 2025 article has an explicitly labeled May 13, 2026 update. It reports identifying Mini Shai-Hulud activity May 11 affecting **170+ npm packages and 2 PyPI packages across 404 malicious versions**, and describes optional git-dependency/prepare execution, Bun payloads, Claude SessionStart persistence and GitHub repository infection. These counts belong to that resurgence, not September or August. Do not repeat its expansive "first" cross-registry claim without a separate historical investigation. [Microsoft](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2025/12/09/shai-hulud-2-0-guidance-for-detecting-investigating-and-defending-against-the-supply-chain-attack/)

### Inferences
- Useful progression: account/token compromise and post-install worm propagation in September; pre-install Bun-based propagation and additional persistence in November; Mini-family developer-tool persistence in May; compromised-source release automation plus enhanced blockchain C2 in August. This is a mechanism comparison, not proof the same operator ran every wave. [Wiz September](https://www.wiz.io/blog/shai-hulud-npm-supply-chain-attack); [Datadog November](https://securitylabs.datadoghq.com/articles/shai-hulud-2.0-npm-worm/); [Microsoft May](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2025/12/09/shai-hulud-2-0-guidance-for-detecting-investigating-and-defending-against-the-supply-chain-attack/); [Wiz August](https://www.wiz.io/blog/keyv-and-cacheable-npm-supply-chain-attack)

### Gaps
- This is not an exhaustive inventory of every 2026 variant. Avoid unqualified ordinal labels such as "sixth attack" without defining the campaign taxonomy.
- September and November do not have a single verified global infection or malicious-download total in the material examined. Do not add their package counts together as a unique cumulative total.

## Is August 4, 2026 ChainDrop real, did OIDC fail, and what could another registry change?

### Takeaway
The incident is real and unusually well supported, including an August 31 maintainer postmortem. It demonstrates compromised source/maintainer authority using legitimate GitHub Actions and OIDC publication, not a demonstrated cryptographic break in trusted publishing. [Jared Wray](https://jaredwray.com/blog/keyv-and-cacheable-chaindrop-worm)

### Cited Findings
- Wray's account: "I mistakenly left an old full-access GitHub token active on a machine that was infected." The detailed account qualifies the conclusion: "Given that two-token inventory, I believe it was the credential used" and "we have not been able to be 100% sure how they got access to that token". Therefore report a maintainer-attributed likely stale-PAT entry point, not a proven original infection mechanism. His raw audit exports are not public. [Postmortem, August 31](https://jaredwray.com/blog/keyv-and-cacheable-chaindrop-worm)
- The postmortem says the attacker pushed into Keyv, Cacheable and Ecto on August 4; `keyv@6.0.0` published at 09:35 UTC; initial carrier versions included eleven package names, with the `@keyv/*` adapter tarballs published before the executable hook remaining clean. "Provenance proved where and how the package was built. It did not prove that the source was safe or that the change was authorized." [Wray](https://jaredwray.com/blog/keyv-and-cacheable-chaindrop-worm)
- The eleven first-wave carriers listed by the maintainer are `keyv@6.0.0`, `@cacheable/net@2.1.1`, `@cacheable/node-cache@3.1.2`, `cacheable@2.5.1`, `flat-cache@6.1.24`, `cacheable-request@13.0.20`, `@cacheable/memory@2.2.1`, `file-entry-cache@11.1.6`, `@cacheable/utils@2.5.1`, `cache-manager@7.2.10`, and `ecto@5.0.1`. [Wray](https://jaredwray.com/blog/keyv-and-cacheable-chaindrop-worm)
- StepSecurity's August 4 post, last updated 18:10 UTC, reports **444 package names and 2,212 versions**, with observations through 13:20 UTC. Its split is eleven original carriers and 433 propagated package names covering 2,201 versions. This is a specific investigator snapshot, not a final ecosystem total. It names the worm ChainDrop. [StepSecurity](https://www.stepsecurity.io/blog/chaindrop-npm-worm)
- Checkmarx's August 5, 20:32 UTC publication reports **443 unique packages and 2,335 versions**. This differs from StepSecurity on both dimensions. The sources do not establish a shared inventory or counting method, so preserve attribution and do not combine the largest number from each into a new claim. Checkmarx provides its own public gist. [Checkmarx](https://checkmarx.com/zero-post/shai-hulud-npm-supply-chain-attack-keyv-cacheable/)
- Wiz independently reports over 400 distinct npm packages, identifies a Mini Shai-Hulud descendant and provides a public exact-version CSV. [Wiz analysis](https://www.wiz.io/blog/keyv-and-cacheable-npm-supply-chain-attack); [Wiz package inventory](https://github.com/wiz-sec-public/wiz-research-iocs/blob/main/reports/keyv-packages.csv)
- Technical chain: npm `preinstall` launches `setup.mjs`; the loader uses/downloads legitimate Bun; bundled `Math_Symbol.js` or `math_init.js` harvests developer, npm, GitHub, cloud and infrastructure secrets; recovered authority is used to republish modified packages. Bun is an abused legitimate runtime, not evidence Bun's own distribution was compromised. [StepSecurity](https://www.stepsecurity.io/blog/chaindrop-npm-worm)
- Ethereum is a C2-domain discovery mechanism, not the destination for the stolen credential data itself. Wiz describes `eth_call` to a `StringListStore` contract, with HTTPS exfiltration and GitHub repositories as fallback. It also documents Claude Code hooks, VS Code tasks, AI credential targeting and a selectively armed credential-revocation watcher. Do not say every infected host necessarily had every persistence technique activated. [Wiz](https://www.wiz.io/blog/keyv-and-cacheable-npm-supply-chain-attack)
- Initial publication used the real repositories' release workflows with valid SLSA provenance and npm OIDC trusted-publisher identity. StepSecurity provides the `keyv@6.0.0` attestation's repository, workflow, commit and run identifier. This is supported by the maintainer. It does not show forged OIDC identity, broken signatures, or npm accepting an unauthorized external publisher. [StepSecurity](https://www.stepsecurity.io/blog/chaindrop-npm-worm); [Wray](https://jaredwray.com/blog/keyv-and-cacheable-chaindrop-worm)
- Do not generalize initial-wave OIDC evidence to every propagated publication. StepSecurity describes a broader publishing engine including OIDC/provenance support; Checkmarx describes propagation through valid npm tokens with publish rights. Presence of an OIDC exchange endpoint in malware is not, by itself, a measured count of successful OIDC propagation. [StepSecurity](https://www.stepsecurity.io/blog/chaindrop-npm-worm); [Checkmarx](https://checkmarx.com/zero-post/shai-hulud-npm-supply-chain-attack-keyv-cacheable/)
- Stronger real-exposure evidence than popularity totals: StepSecurity reports ten non-lab Backstage CI runs contacting the worm's C2, but explicitly says "we found no evidence of credential loss" and that those workflows had no repository secrets, only an ephemeral job token. These are observed execution/network contacts, not ten proven secret-theft victims. [StepSecurity and linked public run evidence](https://www.stepsecurity.io/blog/chaindrop-npm-worm)
- The maintainer reports about seven hours to halt propagation/remove infected releases from his projects and 36 hours to clean all branches. These are attributed recovery claims, not proof every downstream package or developer machine was remediated within that time. [Wray](https://jaredwray.com/blog/keyv-and-cacheable-chaindrop-worm)

### Inferences
- Another registry could reduce registry-specific account takeover and stolen publication-token abuse through phishing-resistant authentication, short-lived/scoped identities, malware quarantine, release cooldowns and a separate release-promotion approval. A proxy could enforce exact-version blocks across an organization. These are control-dependent improvements, not inherent guarantees from changing operators. [GitHub hardening plan](https://github.blog/security/supply-chain-security/our-plan-for-a-more-secure-npm-supply-chain/); [Checkmarx proxy/cooldown guidance](https://checkmarx.com/zero-post/shai-hulud-npm-supply-chain-attack-keyv-cacheable/); [Wray staged-publishing remediation](https://jaredwray.com/blog/keyv-and-cacheable-chaindrop-worm)
- Merely mirroring the same npm tarballs does not remove their malicious source, lifecycle hooks or transitive dependencies. A legitimate source/CI identity can publish malicious source to another registry too. Tokenless publishing removes a durable npm secret, but not a stolen GitHub PAT's ability to change trusted source. Separate source review, artifact approval, install-script policy, runner isolation and credential boundaries are necessary. [Wray](https://jaredwray.com/blog/keyv-and-cacheable-chaindrop-worm); [PostHog](https://posthog.com/blog/nov-24-shai-hulud-attack-post-mortem)

### Gaps
- No standalone official GitHub/npm August incident postmortem was located in this search. The official response is described first-hand by the maintainer; Checkmarx links an npm token-rotation announcement on X, but that announcement was not fetched independently here.
- No exact campaign-wide August malicious-version download count or final verified unique-victim total. Package-level weekly/monthly downloads are potential reach only, and summing them double-counts shared dependency consumption.
- Editor persistence is not unconditional execution in every VS Code or Claude Code installation. Workspace trust, task approvals, installed versions and configuration affect execution; no universal bypass was established here.
- Different registry: it can mitigate publication authentication, distribution and screening risks if its controls genuinely differ. Shared ecosystem risk remains wherever compromised maintainers, malicious source/CI, trusted release identities, transitive code and secret-bearing developer environments are still trusted. [Wray's concrete boundary analysis](https://jaredwray.com/blog/keyv-and-cacheable-chaindrop-worm)
